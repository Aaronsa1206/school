Collisions in 2D

The big idea: split the momentum into x and y, and keep each one separately.

Before the hit, everything moves along x, so the total y-momentum is zero. After the hit it still has to be zero. That's why one ball goes up and the other goes down: their y-momenta cancel. Energy is a third, separate equation, and it only stays the same if the collision is elastic.

Why θ + φ = 90° (Billiard Ball Theorem)

Only when the masses are equal, the target starts at rest, and no energy is lost. Cancel the m's and you get:

momentum: v₁ᵢ = v₁f + v₂f (as arrows) energy: v₁ᵢ² = v₁f² + v₂f²

The first line says the three arrows form a triangle. The second line is a² + b² = c², which only works for a right triangle. So the two "after" arrows are perpendicular. Try the Billiard balls preset and watch the square corner appear in the arrow diagram, then move the mass slider and watch it break.

Counting unknowns (how to tell if you can solve it)

Known: m₁, m₂, v₁ᵢ. Unknown: v₁f, v₂f, θ, φ, ΔE. That's 5 unknowns and 3 equations, so the problem has to hand you 2 of them.

"Elastic" counts as one (it means ΔE = 0). Then you still need one more, like an angle or a final speed. The sim works the same way: you set θ and ΔE, and it solves for the other three.

Check yourself

Two identical pool balls, elastic. The cue ball leaves at 35° above the line. Where does the other ball go?

55° below the line. Equal masses + elastic + target at rest means θ + φ = 90°, so φ = 90° − 35°.

Why is the left side of the y-equation zero?

Before the collision, m₁ moves only along x and m₂ isn't moving. So the total y-momentum before is 0, and momentum conservation says it's still 0 after.

A problem gives m₁, m₂, v₁ᵢ, says the collision is inelastic, and gives θ. Can you solve it?

No. "Inelastic" doesn't give you a number for ΔE, so you only have 1 of the 2 extra facts (θ). You'd need one more, such as v₁f or φ.

Why is there a minus sign in the y-equation?

m₂ goes below the line, so its y-velocity is negative: −v₂f sin φ. Writing the minus sign explicitly lets you keep φ as a positive angle.

Center of mass

The big idea: it's an average position where heavier things count more.

If you put all the masses on a weightless stick, the center of mass is the one spot where you could balance the stick on your finger. Heavy masses pull the balance point toward themselves.

Quick sanity checks

Equal masses: the center of mass lands exactly in the middle. One mass much heavier: it lands almost on top of the heavy one. The answer is always somewhere between the outermost masses. If your answer isn't, you made an arithmetic slip.

Continuous object: a uniform rod

Instead of adding up a few masses, you add up infinitely many thin slices. Each slice has mass dm = λ dx.

λ = M/L (mass per meter) x_cm = (1/M) ∫₀ᴸ x dm = (1/M) ∫₀ᴸ x (M/L) dx = (1/L) · [x²/2]₀ᴸ = (1/L) · L²/2 = L/2 (the middle ✓)

The integral sign is just the Σ from the many-particle formula, done with tiny pieces.

Check yourself

A 2 kg mass is at x = 0 and a 6 kg mass is at x = 4 m. Where's the center of mass?

x_cm = (2·0 + 6·4)/(2+6) = 24/8 = 3 m. It's closer to the 6 kg mass, which makes sense.

Where is the center of mass of a uniform 2 m rod that runs from x = 0 to x = 2?

At x = L/2 = 1 m, the middle. "Uniform" means λ is constant, which is what makes it land at the middle.

Three 1 kg masses sit at (0,0), (2,0), and (0,2). Find (x_cm, y_cm).

Do x and y separately. x_cm = (0+2+0)/3 = 0.67 m. y_cm = (0+0+2)/3 = 0.67 m. So (0.67, 0.67) m.

Systems of many particles

The big idea: total momentum = (total mass) × (velocity of the center of mass).

Take v_cm = Σmᵢvᵢ / M and multiply both sides by M: M·v_cm = Σmᵢvᵢ = p_total. If no outside force acts, p_total can't change, so v_cm can't change either. An explosion only has internal forces, so the pieces fly apart but the center of mass keeps coasting in a straight line at the same speed.

Explode a shell in space no gravity, no outside forces

Watch the green dot. However you set the sliders, it never swerves or speeds up. The light piece always flies off faster than the heavy one, because their momenta have to cancel.

Check yourself

A 4 kg shell drifts through space at 20 m/s and splits into two 2 kg halves. One half stops dead. How fast is the other?

Momentum before: 4·20 = 80 kg·m/s. After: 2·0 + 2·v = 80, so v = 40 m/s. The center of mass is still moving at 20 m/s.

Where does v_cm = Σmᵢvᵢ / M come from?

Take the time derivative of r_cm = Σmᵢrᵢ / M. The masses are constant, so each rᵢ just turns into vᵢ.

A firework explodes at the top of its arc (with gravity). Does the center of mass still go in a straight line?

No. Gravity is an external force, so the center of mass keeps following the same parabola the firework was already on. The explosion still doesn't change that path.

Rocket propulsion

The big idea: the rocket throws mass backward, so the rocket goes forward.

Rocket plus exhaust is one system with no outside force, so its total momentum stays the same. Each bit of exhaust carries momentum backward, and the rocket picks up the same amount forward. The rocket keeps getting lighter, so each kick does more, and that's where the natural log comes from.

Burn the fuel Δv = v_e ln(M_i / M_f)

Notice the curve flattens. Going from a mass ratio of 2 to 4 adds the same Δv as going from 4 to 8, so each extra bit of speed costs a lot more fuel. That's why real rockets drop empty stages.

The derivation, one step at a time

This is the likely "derive this" exam question. Cover the steps and try to write each one yourself before you reveal it.

  1. Set up the picture.

    Rocket of mass M + Δm moving at v. It spits out Δm backward at v_e relative to the rocket, so the exhaust moves at v − v_e as seen from the ground.

  2. Momentum before = momentum after.
    (M + Δm)v = M(v + Δv) + Δm(v − v_e)
  3. Multiply everything out.
    Mv + Δm·v = Mv + MΔv + Δm·v − Δm·v_e
  4. Cancel what's on both sides (Mv and Δm·v).
    0 = MΔv − Δm·v_e → M Δv = v_e Δm
  5. Make it calculus.

    The exhaust mass Δm is mass the rocket loses, so Δm = −dM.

    M dv = −v_e dM → dv = −v_e dM / M
  6. Integrate from start to finish.
    ∫ dv = −v_e ∫ dM/M v_f − v_i = −v_e [ln M_f − ln M_i] v_f − v_i = v_e ln(M_i / M_f)

    Check this against your professor's sign convention in your notes.

Check yourself

v_e = 2000 m/s and the rocket burns until it's 1/4 of its starting mass. What's Δv?

Δv = 2000 · ln(4) = 2000 · 1.386 ≈ 2770 m/s.

Why do we write dM = −Δm?

Δm is the (positive) amount of exhaust thrown out. The rocket's mass M goes down by that much, so the change in the rocket's mass is negative.

Why is the exhaust speed v − v_e and not just −v_e?

v_e is measured from the rocket. The rocket is already moving at v, so from the ground you add the two: v + (−v_e) = v − v_e. Momentum has to be written in one frame (the ground).

Rigid body rotation

The big idea: it's the kinematics you already know, with new letters.

Swap position x for angle θ, velocity v for angular velocity ω, and acceleration a for angular acceleration α. Every formula carries over. To get back to ordinary meters, multiply by the radius r. Angles must be in radians for that to work.

Spin the wheel the dot rides at radius r

Blue v = rω (along the rim). Orange a_c = rω² (toward the center). Ink a_t = rα (along the rim). Set α to a nonzero value and watch ω change.

The last three rows follow the same swap pattern (constant α only). Check that they're on your formula sheet.

Two accelerations, two jobs

a_t = rα changes how fast the dot moves. It's zero if the spin rate is steady.

a_c = rω² changes the direction the dot moves, bending it into a circle. It's never zero while the wheel spins, even at a steady rate.

Why radians?

A radian is defined so that arc length = radius × angle (s = rθ). That only holds in radians, and v = rω and a_t = rα come from taking derivatives of it. Convert first: 1 rev = 2π rad = 360°.

Check yourself

A wheel spins at ω = 4 rad/s. A point sits at r = 0.5 m. Find v and a_c.

v = rω = 0.5·4 = 2 m/s. a_c = rω² = 0.5·16 = 8 m/s². Check: v²/r = 4/0.5 = 8 ✓.

Convert 3 revolutions to radians.

3 · 2π = 6π ≈ 18.85 rad.

A fan speeds up from 0 to 10 rad/s in 5 s. What's α_avg?

α_avg = Δω/Δt = (10 − 0)/5 = 2 rad/s². Same as a = Δv/Δt.

Two kids ride a merry-go-round, one at the edge and one near the middle. Which has the bigger ω? Bigger v?

Same ω, since the whole rigid body turns together. The kid at the edge has the bigger v, because v = rω and their r is larger.
